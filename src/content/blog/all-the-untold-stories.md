I recently watched a talk by Zack Proser called [I Don't Fear AI Slop. I Fear Untold Stories](https://youtu.be/1et3Lb_KLQo). What stuck with me was the thought of people with something worth sharing who never get around to writing it down.

Some don't think of themselves as writers. Some don't know where to start. Whatever the reason, there's a story we never get to read.

I think there's a parallel with software.

## All the software we never build

Whatever you think about AI, there are things we don't build because they take too long, cost too much or require skills we don't have.

An idea might be feasible for a well-funded company with a team of specialists. That doesn't make it feasible for the person who actually has it. Even an experienced developer can have an idea that's well outside the kind of software they know how to build.

Imagine a DevOps engineer with an idea for a game in Unreal Engine. They've got a young family and other things going on. Learning an unfamiliar engine and finding the time to build a prototype might be enough to stop them trying.

Maybe the game would be brilliant. Maybe it wouldn't work at all. Without a chance to try it, they never find out.

Or imagine a doctor who sees a way to improve how their patients receive care. They understand the problem, but don't have the technical skills to build a prototype. AI might help them make something they could show to a developer and discuss.

That's what interests me about AI. It might make that first attempt possible, even if it's rough. Something that would have taken months of spare evenings might become small enough to have a go at.

## The cost of finding out

I sometimes feel frustrated by how familiar software still looks. We still spend a lot of time dealing with email, messages and awkward interfaces. I don't feel as though the software I've used has improved as much as I thought it would. I wonder how many ideas for doing things differently never made it far enough for anyone to try them.

Even when we know how to build something, we're usually working within a budget or a deadline. We choose an approach we can deliver. We don't necessarily get to test an alternative or learn enough about another field to see whether it offers a better way.

<p class="pull-quote">I think we attach too much importance to the code we write.</p>

A polished codebase can still solve the wrong problem. The idea and whether it helps someone matter more. Early on, I'd rather find out whether an idea is useful than perfect or protect the code behind it.

## Software Should Be Cheap

Some of what we make will be good. Some of it will be bad. I think the ideas and the decisions we make have always mattered more than the code itself. As AI makes writing code cheaper, that becomes harder to ignore. What should we build? Who would it help? How do we know it works?

I'd like more people to have a chance to answer those questions, especially the ones who understand a problem but have never had the means to try solving it.

Software should be cheap because the ideas aren't.
